# Unreal Engine 5 Development — Procedural Content Management

There is no denying that content has always been the core of the gaming experience, which is very similar to the film and television industry. However, there are significant differences in the production processes between the gaming and film/television industries. Although games offer excellent interactive feedback that allows players to deeply engage, the strict constraint of computing power, like the Sword of Damocles hanging overhead, greatly restricts content production in the gaming industry.

When creating content, the film and television industry can, to some extent, rely on massive investments of manpower, material resources, and time, adopting a "brute force" approach—pouring in huge resources and achieving high-quality content output through resource accumulation and intensive production.

However, the gaming industry finds it difficult to follow this path. Due to computing power limitations, games cannot endlessly pile up content elements; otherwise, issues like game lag and unstable operation will occur, severely impacting the player experience.

Therefore, before engaging in content production in the gaming industry, solving engineering problems becomes particularly crucial. Only by optimizing engineering processes, rationally applying technical means, and improving resource utilization efficiency can we achieve high-quality, efficient output of game content under limited computing power conditions, meeting players' ever-growing demands.

## Procedural Technology

In the past, procedural techniques were primarily a concept focused on by developers. But in recent years, with the acceleration of game industrialization, they have quietly permeated every link of game content production, like precision gears embedded in a large machine, driving the overall operation efficiently. There is no doubt that procedural technology has become a key driving force for enhancing game content production capacity, injecting a steady stream of vitality into the development of the gaming industry.

The essence of [Procedural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_generation) applications is to store the problem-solving process as data assets, ensuring that subsequent development can rely on these operational data for reuse, iteration, etc. (Conceptually, it is a program, just a program within a program).

Current applications of procedural technology in the gaming industry mainly include:

- [**Procedural Content Generation**](https://docs.unrealengine.com/5.2/en-US/procedural-content-generation-overview/)
- [**Procedural Modeling**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_modeling)
- [**Procedural Textures**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_texture)
- [**Procedural Animation**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_animation)
- [**Procedural Audio**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_media)

These technologies have gradually permeated the daily work of art asset production, with a notable feature: **creating assets through programming rather than just parameter tuning**.

## Procedural Content Management

The application of the procedural technologies mentioned above has endowed us with unprecedented creative efficiency. Compared to the past, we can now produce richer game content in a shorter time and with a lower budget. This not only significantly improves development efficiency but also expands the boundaries of game content, bringing more diverse experiences to players.

However, every coin has two sides. The production of more content also means that management costs will grow exponentially. From asset management and version control to testing and update iteration, each link requires more investment of manpower, material resources, and time costs to ensure that the庞大 content system can operate in an orderly manner without any chaos or errors.

Previously, the author highly recommended using PCG technology for scene construction because integrating a generation pipeline is definitely easier for iteration and optimization.

But in reality, the industry's application of PCG is still relatively idealistic, and there are not many practical application scenarios that can be truly implemented.

A major reason for this is that PCG is not "grounded". Currently, the job responsibilities for scene design in most game production teams are generally divided as follows:

- **Game Planner**: Responsible for planning the overall framework of the game scene, including the scene's functions, story background, style positioning, player experience flow in the scene, etc., providing clear directions and requirement documents for scene design.
- **Concept Designer**: Based on the planner's requirements, conduct concept design of the scene, drawing concept sketches of the overall style, atmosphere, general form of main buildings and terrain through hand-drawing or digital painting, providing visual references for subsequent detailed design.
- **Scene Concept Artist**: On the basis of concept design, conduct detailed concept art design of specific elements in the scene, including the structure of buildings, decorative details, undulating changes of terrain, styles of vegetation and props, etc., presenting the shape, color, material and other details of each element in the form of precise paintings, providing accurate production blueprints for modelers.
- **3D Modeler**: Use 3D modeling software to construct the designed scene elements into 3D models based on scene concept art, including building the basic framework of buildings, terrain, props, etc., endowing them with volume and spatial sense, and performing preliminary UV unwrapping to prepare for subsequent texture painting.
- **Material and Lighting Artist**: Add materials and textures to 3D models to simulate the real texture of objects, such as wood texture, metal luster, stone roughness, etc. At the same time, responsible for setting the lighting effects in the scene, creating different atmospheres and light and shadow effects, enhancing the realism and visual impact of the scene.
- **Scene Sound Designer**: Responsible for creating, selecting, and adding appropriate sound effects for the scene, conducting sound effect design according to the theme, emotion, atmosphere of the scene, and through collection, editing, mixing and other operations, making the sound effects perfectly integrate with the scene, enhancing the immersion of the scene.
- **VFX Artist**: According to the needs of the scene, design and produce various special effects, such as fire, smoke, water flow, magic effects, etc., adding vividness and interest to the scene, and improving the player's immersion.
- **Level Designer**: Mainly responsible for the layout and planning of the scene, reasonably arranging the positions of monsters, props, task points, obstacles and other elements in the scene according to gameplay and planner requirements, designing the player's movement route and level difficulty curve, ensuring that players can obtain a good gaming experience in the scene.
- **Scene Optimizer**: Perform performance optimization on the completed scene, reducing the resource occupation of the scene on hardware by reducing the number of model faces, compressing textures, reasonably setting light baking, etc., improving the running efficiency of the game, and ensuring smooth operation on different devices.

In the process of scene design, close communication and frequent iteration between the above positions are usually required to complete the final delivery.

The reason for using PCG is that after game teams significantly improve the production capacity of content raw materials (models, materials, VFX, sound effects...), they also need an efficient means to complete scene construction while considering performance optimization issues.

However, due to the complexity of PCG pipeline design, multi-person collaboration is very, very difficult. Specifically in actual projects, it may require a high-level TA to be responsible for writing PCG logic. This person must not only be proficient in the art production process, supervise the standardization of raw materials, but also take into account the game planner's requirements, respond to changes in a timely manner, and understand the underlying engine to formulate performance optimization strategies.

![img](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/5a4b6bb9647d7be20323714f1b8a23414e7b9f2b.jpg)

According to [related information](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/the-matrix-awakens-an-unreal-engine-5-experience/154271/67), during the development of Unreal Engine's *The Matrix: Awakens* demo, approximately `20-30` people were responsible for handling assets, and the entire team had `50-70` staff members. This is undoubtedly an absolute feat.

In this demo, we can see the huge potential of PCG: it "easily" built such a magnificent city.

The reason why PCG is difficult to implement can be directly attributed to the fact that many teams can hardly have the same team quality as Epic.

Secondly, the goal of PCG is to summarize and conclude a sustainable iterative production pipeline. But for game products, what attracts players is always content and creativity, which are inevitably unconstrained.

Specifically in project teams, PCG can centralize the responsibilities of multiple positions. It has advantages such as quickly responding to iterations, but also has disadvantages such as limiting the creative play of various personnel, thereby affecting content quality and production capacity.

Unlike PCG, procedural content management is more like a post-processing:

- It aims to use procedural means to make content organization convergent.

Therefore, this article does not focus on introducing those profound cutting-edge technologies, but focuses on sorting out and integrating a series of common procedural means to manage **Objects**, **Assets**, and **Worlds** in Unreal Engine during the current game content production process.

The reason for using procedural means to manage content, simply put, is:

- Some operations that seem very "intuitive" are just difficult to do at the editor level.

This dilemma can be illustrated by a short dialogue:

>A: Do you know elephants?
>
>B: Of course I do.
>
>A: I'm very curious about what it looks like. Can you draw it?
>
>B: Sorry, I can't draw, so I can't~

The purpose of procedural technology is that we do not directly process specific targets, but describe what we want to do through features, so that the input and process of the operation can have a certain degree of flexibility. It is equivalent to giving such a response:

> B: Sorry, although I can't draw an elephant, I can tell you that elephants are very tall, with gray bodies, four thick and strong legs, big ears like palm-leaf fans, long trunks that can roll up things, and two sharp and long teeth. You will definitely recognize it at a glance when you see it later~

For game projects, some heavyweight editors related to content production usually need to be developed and commissioned by professional personnel. But besides that, there are also a large number of trivial **micro-requirements** in the process of game content production. Although it is unnecessary to arrange professional personnel to develop editor tools for these requirements, without the assistance of editor tools, these tasks will be very difficult to complete. For example:

- Select all Actors in the coordinate area `[X0,Y0 - X1,Y1]` and export them as a sub-level for local testing
- Filter out all light sources with shadows enabled in the scene and check the rationality of their shadows
- Overview all mesh components, and batch apply certain lighting and culling LOD strategies to components based on the size of the bounding box and other features
- Overview the bounding box sizes of all scene effects and retrieve whether there are abnormal items in the effect bounding boxes
- Batch adjust properties of specific components, such as displacement transformation, light switch, physics strategy, rendering properties...
- Batch modify the switch status of certain properties of assets
- Batch filter out Actors with specific features and highlight them
- Collect all static models `SM_BigTree0001` in the scene and batch replace them with encapsulated Blueprints `BP_BigTree0001`, which additionally provides particle effect components for falling leaves and sound effect components for leaf friction for the tree model
- Batch send rays to a certain area to sample the scene, and save the scene data obtained from ray detection for making special effects
- Batch preview, sort, search, and edit the `Disallow Nanite` property of `StaticMeshComponent` of all `StaticMeshActors` in the scene
- Automatically merge static meshes in a certain area into a hierarchical instanced static mesh component
- ...

These requirements usually appear frequently in the processes of map editing, scene management, asset management, and performance optimization. In small-scale game projects, since content is accumulated gradually, and people usually have the mentality of "cutting it if it doesn't work", there are generally no major problems. But in large-scale game projects, the relationships between contents are intricate. Although PCG technology can alleviate some pressure, if these micro-requirements are not properly solved in a timely manner, content management is also very easy to get out of control:

Procedural content processing can usually be summarized into three basic steps:

``` mermaid
graph TD
	A(Collection: Collect the objects and data we need by describing features)
	B(Review [Optional]: Review and filter the collected data)
	C(Processing [Optional]: Execute the corresponding operation logic___________)
	A --> B
	B --> C

```

Based on the above process, the author has built a simple and compact editor framework (**ProceduralContentProcessor**) to complete these steps. As a toolbox, it can easily derive and extend editor tools with a unified structure. The plugin's Github repository is:

- https://github.com/Italink/ProceduralContentProcessor

The following text will elaborate on the specific processing steps of each stage based on this plugin.

### Basic Usage

**Procedural Content Processor** itself does not provide many complex mechanisms; it mostly relies on the engine's native functions.

A key point is:

- Blueprint functions with **CallInEditor (Call in Editor)** checked (or C++ functions with the `CallInEditor` identifier in `UFUNCTION`) will generate corresponding buttons on the object's details panel. Clicking this button will execute the corresponding function logic:

![image-20240908160400293](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20240908160400293.png)

Taking this as an opportunity, we can easily write a large number of editor tools.

Some options in the Blueprint **Class Settings** of the procedural content processor will affect the display of the editor outline:

- **Sort Priority**: Sort priority, affecting the sorting of the drop-down box in the editor outline.
- **Document Hyperlink**: The document link of the processor. Clicking the 【? button】 on the editor outline will open the corresponding URL.
- **Override UMGClass**: Use a UMG widget to replace the default details panel, generally used for customizing the editor's UI.
- **Blueprint Display Name**: The actual display name of the processor in the editor outline.
- **Blueprint Description**: The prompt information of the processor in the editor outline.
- **Blueprint Namespace**: The category of the processor in the editor outline. Multi-level categories are separated by `.`.

![image-20240908160746183](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20240908160746183.png)

Some configurations in the Blueprint properties will affect the details panel:

- **Editable Instance**: Only properties with Editable Instance checked can be edited on the object's details panel; otherwise, only the default values can be modified in the Blueprint editor.
- **Read Only Blueprint**: When checked, the property will become uneditable but will be previewed.
- **Config Variable**: When checked, the value of the property will be stored in the configuration file, and the previous configuration will be read when the processor is started next time.

![image-20240908163158884](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20240908163158884.png)

> During editor production, some property identifiers and metadata are very useful, but they are not exposed to the Blueprint editor. Here is a recommended plugin: https://github.com/DoubleDeez/MDMetaDataEditor

The procedural content processor has four basic event functions that can be overridden:

- `void Activate()`: Called when the processor is activated.
- `void Tick(DeltaTime)`: Called every frame after the processor is activated.
- `void Deactivate()`: Called when the processor exits.
- `void PostEditChangeProperty(PropertyName)`: Called when the processor's properties change.
