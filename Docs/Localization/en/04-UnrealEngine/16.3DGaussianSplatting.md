**3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)** is a computer graphics technology for real-time radiance field rendering. It has been a hot topic in academic research in recent years and is widely used in the field of 3D reconstruction.

## 3D Reconstruction Technology

Most technical fields emerge from the combined effects of **technological breakthroughs reducing costs**, **demand upgrades creating markets**, **academic research providing theories**, and **industrial transformations expanding scenarios**.

Against the backdrop of the current explosion in computer technology, the development of the 3D reconstruction field stems from the urgent demand for 3D information across various industries:

- **Industrial Manufacturing: Upgrading from "Manufacturing" to "Intelligent Manufacturing"**

    - **Reverse Engineering**: Acquire data of existing parts through 3D scanning to quickly reconstruct CAD models, shortening the R&D cycle of new products (e.g., mold design for automotive body panels);

    - **Quality Inspection**: Compare reconstructed models with design drawings to detect surface defects or assembly errors (e.g., high-precision inspection of aerospace components);

    - **Digital Twins**: Build 3D virtual models of production lines to monitor equipment status in real time and optimize processes, promoting the implementation of intelligent manufacturing.

- **Medical Health: Precision Medicine and Visualization Revolution**

    - **3D Medical Imaging**: Generate 3D models of organs and tumors from CT/MRI tomographic images to assist doctors in determining lesion locations and surgical paths (e.g., preoperative planning in neurosurgery);

    - **Biomechanical Simulation**: Simulate joint movements based on personalized bone models of patients to develop customized prosthetics or rehabilitation plans.

- **Cultural Heritage and Urban Management: Digital Preservation and Smart Governance**
    - **Cultural Relic Protection and Restoration**: High-precision 3D reconstruction (e.g., the digitalization project of the Mogao Grottoes in Dunhuang) permanently preserves the details of cultural heritage, providing a basis for virtual restoration of damaged cultural relics;
    
    - **Smart City Modeling**: Reconstruct urban building clusters through drone aerial surveys or vehicle-mounted LiDAR to support applications such as traffic planning and disaster simulation (e.g., flood inundation analysis).
    
- **Entertainment and Interaction: Technical刚需 for Immersive Experiences**

    - **Game and Film Industrialization**: Efficient modeling, virtual production, photorealistic rendering, and collaborative production drive the transformation of game and film industrialization toward automated, high-quality, cross-platform collaborative digital production models;

    - **VR/AR Interaction**: Real-time 3D environment reconstruction is the core of SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping), supporting spatial perception and virtual-real fusion in head-mounted devices such as Meta Quest.

- ...

For 3D reconstruction algorithms, the industry usually classifies them based on whether the sensor actively emits light to the object:

- **Active**: Actively project structured light (e.g., laser, fringe light, coded light) or sound waves onto the object, use sensors to receive signals reflected from the object surface, and finally analyze the signals to obtain 3D information of the object. Common methods include laser scanning, Moiré fringe method, structured light method, ToF (Time of Flight), phase measurement profilometry, etc.
- **Passive**: Based on visual geometry principles, collect 2D images of the object through monocular or multi-view cameras, then analyze the images to obtain 3D information of the object. Common methods include stereo vision, SfM+MVS, optical flow method, monocular depth estimation (e.g., deep learning methods), etc.

The main differences between them are as follows:

| **Dimension**       | **Active 3D Reconstruction**                 | **Passive 3D Reconstruction**                         |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hardware Cost**    | High (requires active light source, dedicated sensors) | Low (only requires a camera)                          |
| **Precision**        | High (sub-pixel level, suitable for high-precision requirements) | Medium-low (depends on texture and viewing angle, suitable for macro scenes) |
| **Speed**            | Fast (single frame or few images, suitable for real-time applications) | Slow (multi-view matching, requires offline computation) |
| **Texture Dependence** | Low (actively projects texture)              | High (depends on the object's own texture)            |
| **Dynamic Adaptability** | Poor (sensitive to motion)                   | Good (some algorithms can handle dynamic objects in static scenes) |
| **Typical Scenarios**| Industrial inspection, high-precision modeling of cultural relics | Consumer-grade scanning, drone surveying, large scene reconstruction |

Among them, the development of passive methods is closely related to computer graphics:

- The 1993 SIGGRAPH paper [*View interpolation for image synthesis*](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/166117.166153) first proposed the concept of multi-view interpolation for image synthesis, defining the view synthesis problem and leading the field of image-based rendering (IBR). At that time, computer graphics rendering was limited by the quality of geometric, lighting, and material input models. IBR provided an alternative method to create new images by combining photorealistic input images. Subsequently, work related to view synthesis and image-based rendering grew rapidly, with classic papers on light field rendering, light field maps, layered depth maps, etc.

- The 2020 ECCV paper [*NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis*](https://www.matthewtancik.com/nerf) proposed a neural network-based approach that can generate high-quality novel view images from a small number of 2D images, opening a new chapter in 3D reconstruction. NeRF uses a fully connected neural network (multi-layer perceptron, MLP) to represent 3D scenes. It queries 5D coordinates along camera rays and uses volume rendering techniques to project output colors and densities onto images to synthesize views. It also introduces positional encoding to enable the MLP to recover high-frequency functions and produce high-quality results.

- The 2023 SIGGRAPH paper [*3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering*](https://repo-sam.inria.fr/fungraph/3d-gaussian-splatting/) proposed a scene representation method using 3D Gaussian distributions as graphical primitives. It is an important breakthrough after NeRF, combining rendering efficiency and reconstruction quality, and quickly became a hot topic in academic research.

In the film and game industry, **Mesh** is usually used as the mainstream rendering carrier for graphics, which has the following prominent features: **Primitive** (usually triangles) + **Texture**

![image-20250517000418286](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517000418286.png)

This is because the structure of meshes is highly flexible and easy for iterative editing, which makes the development of modern graphics rendering pipelines inseparable from it. A large number of mature toolchains have been derived, forming standardized industry workflows.

![img](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/the-best-dcc-software-for-3d-animation.webp)

However, although the expression method of separating **geometric precision** and **material precision** in meshes has many advantages, it also brings many complex problems. For example, meshes usually require manual or algorithmic pre-assumptions about the object's topological structure (e.g., vertex connection relationships, surface types, etc.).

The task of graphics rendering is to convert graphics into images, while 3D reconstruction is its inverse solution process (image -> graphics). Therefore, **Point Cloud** is used as the basis for data collection and processing. It is easier to record **unbiased sampling** data of the reconstructed scene and retain original geometric information (e.g., edges, holes, non-manifold structures).

![PointCloud](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/PointCloud.gif)

The point cloud shown above is generated using the open-source tool **[Colmap](https://github.com/colmap/colmap)** with **SFM (Structure from Motion)** + **MVS (Multi-View Stereo)**. We only need to input images from multiple perspectives:

![image-20250517133742847](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517133742847.png)

The SFM process will execute the following steps in sequence:

- **Feature Extraction**: Load input images and perform preprocessing to extract feature points contained in each image.

![image-20250517134831116](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517134831116.png)

- **Feature Matching**: Find the same feature points between different images and establish corresponding relationships.

    ![image-20250517134812663](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517134812663.png)

- **Incremental Reconstruction (Mapper)**: Gradually recover camera poses and sparse point clouds based on matching relationships.

    ![image-20250517134023092](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517134023092.png)

The MVS process further processes the **Sparse Point Cloud** obtained from SFM to get a **Dense Point Cloud**, which includes the following steps:

- **Depth Map Estimation (Stereo)**: Generate a pixel-wise depth map for each input image, i.e., estimate the distance from each pixel-corresponding scene point to the camera.

    ![image-20250517134738523](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517134738523.png)

- **Depth Map Fusion**: Merge depth maps from multiple images into a globally consistent dense point cloud, eliminating conflicts and filling holes.

![image-20250517134707029](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517134707029.png)

However, for the same graphic structure, point clouds usually have a huge data volume. The original model of the above scene has `4,876` triangles, but after converting to a lower-precision point cloud, it still contains `429,113` points. Therefore, the 3D reconstruction field still needs to consider converting point clouds to meshes. Here are some common methods:

- **Poisson Reconstruction**: Treat the point cloud as sampling points of a signed distance function (implicit surface), and extract the isosurface (i.e., mesh surface) by solving the Poisson equation.
- **Moving Least Squares (MLS)**: Fit the tangent plane of each point through local weighted least squares to generate an implicit surface, then extract the mesh using the Marching Cubes algorithm.
- **Delaunay Triangulation**: Generate a triangular mesh in 2D or 3D space by maximizing the empty sphere criterion (Delaunay condition), corresponding to tetrahedral subdivision in 3D scenes.
- **Ball Pivoting Algorithm**: Roll a ball of fixed radius on the point cloud surface. The intersection points of the ball and the point cloud form triangles. Adjust the ball radius to control mesh density and details.
- **Marching Cubes Algorithm**: For each voxel unit (cube), find predefined isosurface patterns based on whether vertices are inside or outside the surface (determined by distance function or density function) to generate triangles. Common strategies include TSDF (Truncated Signed Distance Function).
- **Deep Learning Methods**: Use neural networks to directly predict mesh vertices and topology from point clouds, suitable for automated processing or complex shape reconstruction. Examples include [AtlasNet](https://github.com/ThibaultGROUEIX/AtlasNet), [PointToMesh](https://github.com/nywang16/Pixel2Mesh), [Mesh R-CNN](https://github.com/facebookresearch/meshrcnn), [ConvONet](https://github.com/autonomousvision/convolutional_occupancy_networks), etc.

## 3D Gaussian Splatting

Academically, 3DGS can be regarded as a transformation and expansion of the traditional SFM + MVS process.

It can use SFM results as input, adopt **3D Gaussian distributions** as graphical primitives, and use **Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)** to approximate the original scene, thereby obtaining high-quality graphic effects. The process looks like this:

![image-20250517141337442](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517141337442.png)

A 3D Gaussian primitive takes a 3D spatial point as the center, and its probability density (or radiance intensity) decays exponentially with increasing distance from the center. This may sound abstract, but you can simply imagine a 3D Gaussian distribution as a **colored semi-transparent ellipsoid**.

Taking the above figure as an example, if the "ellipsoid" **does not follow a 3D Gaussian distribution** and is **opaque**, the graphic will look like this:

![image-20250517152824546](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250517152824546.png)

Then restore transparency and 3D Gaussian distribution in sequence:

![PointCloudss](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/PointCloudss.gif)

This makes it easy to understand what a 3D Gaussian distribution is. Defining a 3D Gaussian point mainly uses the following parameters:

- **Center Position (X, Y, Z)**: The center position of the Gaussian point in 3D space.
- **Covariance Matrix (Mat3X3)**: Controls the morphological structure (fatness/thinness) of the Gaussian ellipsoid.
- **Color Parameters (R, G, B)**: Controls the color of the ellipsoid.
- **Opacity**: Controls the opacity of the ellipsoid.

The training process of Gaussian Splatting ultimately generates a `*.ply` file that actually stores a bunch of such Gaussian points. The overall process can be briefly summarized as:

- The input process uses a set of point clouds with basic morphological structures (e.g., sparse point clouds obtained from SFM) as the initial positions of Gaussian primitives.
- During iteration, 3D Gaussian primitives are projected onto the 2D image plane, weights and losses are calculated, and all Gaussian parameters (position, color, covariance, opacity) are updated through backpropagation using stochastic gradient descent.
- During iteration, the number of Gaussian points is dynamically adjusted, low-contribution primitives are eliminated, and densification is performed in high-error regions.

Considering parameter control and optimization during gradient descent, the covariance matrix and color parameters use alternative expressions. The covariance matrix is decomposed as follows:

![img](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/1v5zbNg4P-gpY642L76bAPg.png)

- S is a diagonal scaling matrix, represented by 3 scaling values. Note that during actual training, to keep the scale parameter always positive (since length cannot be negative) and facilitate network optimization, the scale is usually stored in logarithmic form, meaning:
    - The network output / stored in the Ply file is `log(scale)`
    - When used, the exponential `exp(log(scale))` needs to be taken to restore the actual positive scale.
- R is a rotation matrix, represented by a quaternion.

The color parameters use **Spherical Harmonics (SH) instead of directly storing RGB**, which allows the color of each Gaussian sphere to change with the viewing direction, showing richer surface reflections and lighting effects, making rendering more realistic.

If the viewing direction is not considered, converting SH to RGB is very simple:

```python
C0 = 0.28209479177387814
def SH2RGB(sh):
    return sh * C0 + 0.5
```

In addition, for numerical stability, gradient calculation, etc., the opacity is usually not stored directly as normalized opacity but as **logits**:

- The network output / stored in the Ply file is `logit(opacity)`
- When used, `1.0f / (1.0f + exp(-opacity))` is needed to get the real opacity.

So far, we have understood the input, process, and output of 3D Gaussian Splatting. One reason why 3DGS is so popular is its high rendering efficiency.

Because the 3D Gaussian distribution is differentiable, we use a set of parameters to represent the "ellipsoid" in 3D space. When rendering, it is projected onto the screen space and becomes a 2D **ellipse**, which still follows a 2D Gaussian distribution. This means we can derive the shape structure of the ellipse in **screen/image space** based on **3D Gaussian parameters** and **camera parameters** and perform rasterization directly, which can greatly reduce the computational load required for graphic rendering.

However, due to the semi-transparent nature of Gaussian primitives, it is essential to sort them semi-transparently according to the camera during data processing.

Currently, there are three main strategies for rendering 3DGS:

- [**diff-gaussian-rasterization**](https://github.com/graphdeco-inria/diff-gaussian-rasterization): The official 3DGS rasterization library using Cuda.
- [**3DGS.cpp**](https://github.com/shg8/3DGS.cpp): A 3DGS rasterization library implemented through the Vulkan compute pipeline, referencing the Cuda version.
- Using the graphics rendering pipeline, the specific steps are: treat Gaussian primitives as planar quadrilaterals at the geometric level, and draw 2D Gaussian distributions in the shading stage. This approach has high versatility, with some open-source references:
    - [**fast-gaussian-rasterization**](https://github.com/dendenxu/fast-gaussian-rasterization): A version implemented using Python OpenGL with geometry shaders.
    - **[splat](https://github.com/jakobbbb/splat)**: A version implemented using C++ OpenGL through instanced rendering.
    - **[QEngineUtilities](https://github.com/Italink/QEngineUtilities/blob/main/Source/Core/Source/Private/Render/Component/QGaussianSplattingPointCloudRenderComponent.cpp)**: Contains a version implemented using C++ QRhi through instanced rendering.

The above is all about 3DGS. If you are interested in mathematical details, here is a good resource:

- Comprehensive Overview of Gaussian Splatting: https://medium.com/data-science/a-comprehensive-overview-of-gaussian-splatting-e7d570081362

Compared with traditional graphical primitives, 3DGS has many disadvantages:

![image-20250519151322194](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250519151322194.png)

- When expressing graphics with high precision and rich surface effects, the number of Gaussian primitives is similar to or even more than that of triangles.
- Due to the semi-transparent nature of Gaussian primitives, some culling optimization methods become ineffective, and the primitives need to be sorted semi-transparently during rendering.
- Gaussian point clouds are not aligned to the model surface and are discontinuous, containing only color features, which makes it difficult to achieve the lighting effects of graphics or only work in a high-loss way.

These disadvantages are essentially determined by the **mathematical properties of Gaussian distribution** and **gradient descent optimization strategy**. Although the academic community has conducted extensive exploration on these issues, many people still criticize 3DGS as only useful for writing papers.

Returning to the industry, computer graphics technology has undergone revolutionary evolution in recent decades, from early polygon modeling to today's next-generation rendering. Breakthroughs in **precision, efficiency, and realism** have directly shaped the evolution of gaming experiences:

![IGN's top ten open world games of all time. Thoughts? : r/gaming](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/igns-top-ten-open-world-games-of-all-time-thoughts-v0-bhrwxnnt9nm81.png)

This is not only due to the rapid development of hardware devices but also inseparable from the wide application of three core computer technologies in the gaming process:

- **LOD (Level of Detail)**: **"Hierarchical Response, Dynamic Adaptation"**
    - Dynamically adjust processing precision according to the **Significance** of the target object, finding the optimal balance between resource consumption and effect.
- **Culling**: **"Targeted Filtering, Precise Focus"**
    - Quickly identify and exclude elements that do not need to be processed through preset rules, reducing system redundant overhead.
- **Streaming**: **"On-Demand Acquisition, Real-Time Scheduling"**
    - Treat resources as continuous data streams, dynamically loading/unloading according to real-time needs, thereby breaking through physical resource limitations.

As developers pursue higher game graphics and complex logic, to break through the limitations of hardware devices and optimize existing software technology solutions, another technology has emerged — **Imposter**:

- A general methodology to achieve system optimization through **abstract substitution**, whose core is **using a sufficiently good simplified model to replace complex originals in a specific context**.

This idea spans multiple fields such as graphics, distributed systems, and AI, becoming a key tool for building high-performance, elastic systems.

Imposter technology continues to find the optimal solution between "limited resources" and "unlimited experience", becoming an invisible accelerator for modern game development. It usually brings significant reductions in computational load and memory usage, which is very important for real-time graphics systems. Here are some applications of Imposter technology in the game industry:

- **LOD Optimization**: Use Billboard, Flipbook, Mesh Cards, Octahedron, etc., to generate Imposters of static models, reducing the rendering overhead of distant objects.

- **Crowd Optimization**: Degrade non-critical NPCs in the distance (e.g., distant passersby) into static instanced meshes, and bake skeletal animations into textures to achieve dense crowd effects.

- **Culling Optimization**: Use low-polygon Imposters instead of bounding boxes to build a specialized GPU occlusion culling pipeline, improving occlusion culling efficiency.
- **Shadow Optimization**: Pre-bake the self-shadow of graphic objects, use Imposters to generate the cast shadow of the instance, thereby greatly reducing the execution overhead of the shadow process.
- ...

3DGS is likely another derivative of the Imposter scheme in LOD optimization.

In the traditional LOD production process, the industry generally uses **simplification** to generate model LODs:

![image-20250520120552279](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250520120552279.png)

Usually, a simplification rate of `50%` can better balance performance and viewing quality, but this strategy does not yield ideal results for models with complex detail features and irregular geometric shapes.

For example, a model with one million triangles usually requires `14 levels of LOD` if we follow a 50% simplification efficiency, which obviously generates a large amount of data and high LOD scheduling overhead. To limit the number of LODs to within `8`, the simplification efficiency must be increased to `75%`, like this:

![image-20250520162715472](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250520162715472.png)

To ensure smoother visual changes when switching LODs, the interval between LOD chains must be increased, which will significantly increase the operating overhead of the entire graphics system.

Therefore, introducing Imposter can solve this problem well. Common Imposter strategies include:

- **Billboard**: Render the model from a specific angle onto a texture.
- **Flipbook**: Render the model from multiple angles onto a texture, and finally switch the displayed texture according to the player's viewing angle in the material.
- **Billboard Cloud/ Mesh Card Cloud**: Render the model on many mesh cards to stack a viewing effect similar to the source model.
- **Octahedron**: Render the model with a certain viewing angle distribution onto a texture, and finally interpolate images from multiple angles in the material to present a reliable viewing angle transition effect.

![image-20250520164054339](../../../04-UnrealEngine/Resources/image-20250520164054339.png)

For a comparison of these strategies, it was briefly mentioned in the previous article on vegetation optimization. See:

- https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/713731229

These strategies have different advantages and disadvantages and are suitable for different usage requirements. Although they can significantly reduce performance overhead, they are usually only suitable for single models with relatively "symmetrical" structures. Gaussian point clouds have another advantage over meshes:

- Gaussian point clouds are discontinuous feature points, unlike triangles that require continuous topology.

Therefore, the LOD scheme for Gaussian point clouds is very easy to implement. You only need to sort the Gaussian points according to the importance of features, and dynamically scale the Gaussian points that need to be drawn in the current viewport through GPU scheduling when finally displaying.

In fact, the current implementation still has a lot of room for optimization:

- 3DGS training requires a point cloud with basic geometric shapes as input, which currently comes from Colmap's SFM reconstruction. Since the graphics themselves are known to the engine, the Colmap stage can be bypassed to generate point clouds directly.
- The LOD effect focuses more on the edges and corners of the graphics, which can be used to optimize the densification path of 3DGS.

- Although the Niagara implementation has good versatility, state management, memory scheduling, and rendering paths are not optimal.

- Currently, the open-source `Spz` compression algorithm is used on the CPU with a compression ratio of about 10x, but there are some compression schemes using Cuda in the academic community that can achieve a compression ratio close to 100x.
